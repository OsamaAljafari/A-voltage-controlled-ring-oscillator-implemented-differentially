# A-voltage-controlled-ring-oscillator-implemented-differentially
This project is on the topic of ring oscillators, specifically voltage controlled ring oscillators. The topology is very interesting (differential topology), and presents some advantages over the typical CMOS inverter ring, even though they behave similarly on a lot of things (e.g Violation of classic Barkhausen criterion).
The project was made independently and took 4 months, but the report might seem weird. This is because it was submitted for a course which required a discrete implementation. However, this actually exposes a very beautiful fact of the ring topology, is that it's scalable. Behzad Razavi writes that the ring oscillator was made using vacuum tubes in ~1920 (according to my memory). Also, the differential amplifier based ring can have another control terminal, that of the current source at the bottom. A classical CMOS inverter ring oscillator or a single ended ring oscillator (NMOS inverter with resistive load) usually have one control knob.
The differential amplifier topology is not a completely new innovation. Razavi already addresses it but he addresses it in an inredibly complex way (40nm transistor instead of 180nm, FOM calculation etc, phase noise improvement, quadrature oscillator etc.). I hope I could read Razavi's paper now and fully understand it.
There is a very interesting hypothesis which I hadn't had the chance to formally and academically test. It is the speculation in the "ring IC with varactor version". It speculates that the square non-linearity of the current (current proportional to square of overdrive), cancels with the square root non-linearity of the varactor's capacitance with respect to voltage. It is laid out in more detail. Cj0 of the varactor is recorded in the "varactor tuning" file, if anyone is interested in exploring it formally. The sizes of the transistors in the differential pair are L = 180nm and W = 20um, while the current providing transistor has sizing of W = 40u and L = 600n. The rationale behind sizing the tail transistor this way is (1) Increase the output resistance by increasing L. (2) Increasing W to compensate for undesirable effects of increasing the L. What are they? If W is not scaled and L is scaled, then we suffer an overdrive voltage increase. Since we require the tail to be in saturation to provide current, that means that the VDS required to saturate the transistor is larger. This obviously causes some very bad effects and might ruin everything. This is formally said as "output swing limitation". What about the differential pair sizing? It just follows the standard 180n process. This means that a good way forward is to explore the effects of changing the sizes of these diff. pair transistors.
As for phase noise, it may seem very bad for a ring. Two points: (1) A differential ring has better phase noise than the regular ones. (2) Phase noise is traded with power consumption. This leads to another improvement direction; phase noise and power consumption optimization.

Here are some incredibly helpful resources that have benefitted me on this remarkable journey:
1- "RF CMOS ICs" by Thomas Lee. This book helped me understand the violation of the classical barkhausen criterion, and the inherent non-linearity of the ring oscillator. It also introduced me to the essential frequency equation in terms of propagation delay. It mentions the ISF phase noise model which I haven't had time to explore, and I just described it briefly as weighted "hits" on different parts of a signal due to time variance. It also enlightened me on concepts like the Q factor and how they apply to oscillators, e.g LC. The concept of Q factor has to be one of the important topics which are covered in "rigor" and meaningless equations, when the actual rigor is to explain it first as a measure of how much energy loss is experienced by an inductor as a sinusoidal current passes it, then developing an equation based on this intuition. This might seem not obvious to the student at all, since they are used to the concept that only resistive loads lose energy. This leads to immediate confusion when exposed to "reactive power".
The phase noise is proportional to the inverse of the square of Q. Meaning that; increase Q, and wow, you get a better phase noise. But.... the ring oscillator doesn't have an immediate "high-Q resonator", unlike the LC. The LC is based upon the concept of "preserving the energy that is lost through an LC load via some sort of negative resistance". A negative impedance converter can be used, yes. But a cross-coupled pair with -2/gm resistance can be better, when dealing with RF (obviously there are some new op amps by texas instruments that can have a GBW of 8000MHz!! https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/opa855-q1.pdf?ts=1777055597851&ref_url=https%253A%252F%252Fwww.ti.com%252Fproduct-category%252Famplifiers%252Fop-amps%252Fhigh-speed%252Fproducts.html   However, I don't have the knowledge about other performance parameters like slew rate etc. to properly analyze such an op amp).

2- "https://electronics.stackexchange.com/questions/672700/ring-oscillator" This phenomenal electrical stack exchange post also helped me understand why a single CMOS inverter in a feedback loop fails to oscillate, even though it achieves essentially the same phase shift as any odd number ring oscillator. Some users also proposed an interesting inductive feedback (to introduce an artifical delay, rings work on delay), which might be worth exploring.

Note: The differential pair analysis and transfer function derivation may seem hand-wavy but it is based on the half circuit model of a diff. pair. I advise checking it out before embarking on reading the loop gain. I would argue that loop gain is the most difficult part of the analysis, but it is absolutely enlightening and revealing of the world of non-linear and delay based oscillators.
